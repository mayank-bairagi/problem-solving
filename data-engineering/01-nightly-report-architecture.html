The Seven-Hour Report

A nightly per-city report drifted from 20 minutes to seven hours without anyone touching the query. Here is what actually broke, and the architecture that fixes it — roles first, products second.

Step 0

Diagnose before designing

“Nobody changed the query” is the clue. What changed is the history, not the workload. The trips table has no partitioning, so a query about yesterday reads three years to find it.

Decision 0 — the shape of the work

A bigger machine buys roughly 2× against 40%-a-year growth. The fix is structural: read only the day in question, and stop re-reading history. Everything below is consequence.

The shape

Five boxes, and what fills them

Each box is named by role first. The product underneath it is a second, separable decision — swap any one of them without moving the boxes.

Step 1

The boundary: what leaves the operational store

Why a seven-hour analytical query against a live replica is dangerous — four separate mechanisms, not one:

  • It pins a read snapshot for seven hours. The engine must retain every row version created in that window — bloat on the primary, growing replica lag.
  • Rows mutate under the scan. Either the snapshot holds and you pay the point above, or the scan sees a smear of states and two runs disagree with no explanation.
  • Schema coupling. An app-team column rename silently breaks the nightly report.
  • Contention. It competes with the application's own reads on the same machine.

Ranked against a stated test: can it reconstruct all four metrics, does it capture the 14-day corrections, and what does it cost the application team?

Decision 1 — Change Data Capture

Read the database's own transaction log rather than its tables. Near-zero query load, captures every insert, update and delete — including back-office fare corrections — and asks nothing of application developers.

On event sourcing: it is the better model — immutable, with requested_at and arrived_at as first-class facts rather than mutable columns. But it has a hole for exactly this problem: a refund issued by a back-office tool never passes through the application, so an event stream misses the correction case that makes this scenario hard. Strategic target, not the thing that ships.

Tech: Debezium as a Kafka Connect source connector, Kafka as transport, the Confluent S3 sink connector writing to the landing zone. Debezium emits a before/after envelope per change with the source commit timestamp — which is what makes the restatement logic in Step 5 possible at all.

Step 2

Landing: a log of changes, not a picture of state

Decision 2a — store changes, not state

A state snapshot overwrites history; a change log keeps it. This is the only layer that can answer “what did the report say last Tuesday, and why”, and the only one that can rebuild every layer below it after a transform bug.

Decision 2b — partition by ingest date, not trip date

Ingest date is the only thing known at write time. Writes stay sequential, and a failed load becomes trivially idempotent: drop the ingest partition, re-land it.

Decision 2c — no business logic here

Land it as received. Every transformation applied at this layer is one you can never undo.

Tech: S3, raw Debezium envelopes in Avro. One operational catch your Kafka Connect answer creates: a sink flushing every few minutes produces thousands of small objects per day, and a year of them makes listing and reading expensive. Set the sink's flush size and rotation interval deliberately, and run a compaction job that rewrites each closed ingest partition into a few large objects.

Step 3

Modeled: collapse changes into queryable trips

Decision 3a — partition by trip_date

Every access pattern filters on date: nightly is one partition, an arbitrary past day is one, month-to-date is ~30, year-to-date is 365. This is the decision that kills the 17.5 TB scan.

Decision 3b — do not also partition by city; sort by it

City partitioning gives 300 × 365 = 109,500 partitions a year, and the skew makes it worse — the top city's file is roughly 6× the median while a hundred cities produce near-empty ones. Sorting by city inside the date partition gets contiguous per-city reads with none of the small-file or skewed-task cost.

Decision 3c — columnar storage

The job touches about 5 of ~30 columns. Row storage reads all 400 bytes to use 40. With Parquet plus compression, a day drops from 16 GB to roughly 3–4 GB actually read.

Decision 3d — compute wait_seconds once, here

A derived column computed in the query is computed every time, by every consumer, eventually differently.

The box you mapped around

Your mapping was Spark + Parquet. Spark answers who does the work; Parquet answers how bytes sit on disk. Neither answers what happens when a rebuild and a reader touch the same day at once — which is the situation Decision 5a creates every single night.

That third question needs a table format: Iceberg, Delta Lake or Hudi. It is the layer that turns a pile of Parquet files in an S3 prefix into a table with atomic commits.

Tech: Spark for the collapse (MERGE INTO against the Debezium change log), writing an Iceberg table in Parquet on S3, catalogued in Glue or an Iceberg REST catalog. Iceberg over Delta chiefly for partition evolution — if the date-partition choice ever proves wrong, it can be changed without rewriting three years of history.

Step 4

Serving: 300 rows a day, plus two sketches

Rides and revenue are distributive — a slice hands back one number and you add them. Unique riders and p95 are holistic: no fixed-size per-slice summary exists, so month-to-date and year-to-date cannot be computed from daily rows at all.

Decision 4a — store sketches alongside the numbers, not instead of them

The exact distinct count and exact p95 are correct and free at day grain, so single-day views are served from those. The HLL and t-digest exist only to make MTD/YTD possible. Exact where exactness is free; estimated only where it is the price of the question.

Tech, with a real trap: Spark's approx_count_distinct and percentile_approx return numbers, not storable sketches — useless for this. Use Spark 3.5's hll_sketch_agg / hll_union_agg (Apache DataSketches under the hood, serialized to bytes) and a DataSketches KLL or t-digest aggregate for the quantile. If Trino is the query engine, verify that the sketch binary it reads is the same family it was written with — Trino's native HyperLogLog and tdigest types are its own formats, and cross-engine compatibility here must be tested, never assumed. Pick one sketch library and make every engine agree on it, because Decision 4a's third price is that the format is frozen for all history.

Step 5

Restatement, and what the SLA actually promises

Decision 5a — rebuild the trailing 14 days every night

Not just yesterday. Affordable precisely because Step 3 made one day cheap: ~50 GB columnar instead of 17.5 TB.

Decision 5b — publish as_of and a provisional flag on every serving row

Days inside the correction window are labelled on the dashboard. This converts a silent correctness bug into a visible, explained property.

Decision 5c — rewriting a day is an atomic commit

Never an append, never an update in place. This is what makes a retry safe, and it is the requirement that forced the table format in Step 3.

Step 6

Orchestration

Decision 6a — trigger on data availability, not on the clock

A watermark — “all changes with commit time ≤ yesterday 23:59:59 have landed” — starts the run, with a deadline alarm as fallback. A 01:00 cron silently computes an incomplete day the first night the stream lags.

Decision 6b — every step idempotent, keyed by logical date

Re-running a step must produce the same result, never doubled rows. This is the thing that must not happen on retry.

Decision 6c — backfill is the same DAG with a different date range

No separate backfill code path. A second path is a second set of bugs and a guaranteed divergence between what history says and what today says.

Decision 6d — quality gates before publish

Row count within tolerance of the trailing average, no city missing, revenue non-null. Then publish atomically, so nobody ever queries a half-built day.

Tech: Airflow — a sensor on the landing watermark, then land → model (yesterday plus the 14-day window) → aggregate → validate → publish, every task keyed by logical_date so a backfill is airflow dags backfill over the same code.

This is Decision 2b doing visible work. T6 sits under ingest_date=2026-09-12 even though it is a 09-11 trip, because arrival time is the only thing known at write time. Nothing here is ever updated: the refund is a new object, not an edit to part-0001. Four small files from ten records is also the small-file problem in miniature — at real volume a compaction job rewrites each closed day.

Rides did not move — a refund is not an un-ride. Revenue did, two days after the day was first reported, which is exactly what provisional was warning about. An analyst querying at 02:16 that morning saw the complete v41 and then the complete v42; never a mixture, and never a partially rewritten partition. Had the job died at 02:16, v42 would simply not exist and v41 would still be current.

Result

Where the seven hours went

The nightly run now reads fourteen recent day-partitions in columnar form — roughly 50 GB, not 17.5 TB. Minutes inside a 7.5-hour window. More importantly, cost is now proportional to days rebuilt, which is constant, rather than history retained, which grows forever.

One honest caveat

16 GB a day is not big data. Once partitioning and columnar storage are in place, a single machine can run this. What justifies distributed tooling here is three-year reprocessing, concurrent analyst queries, and headroom for growth — not the nightly volume. Reaching for a cluster before fixing the data layout would have bought a 10× speedup on a problem that had a 1000× fix sitting in the partition key.
